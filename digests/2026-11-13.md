## Universal Infectious Disease Forecasting with Mantis 2

**Authors:** Carson Dudley, Reiden Magdaleno, Yutong Bi, Anthony Valencia, Xiaofeng Liu, Marisa Eisenberg

**Primary category:** cs.AI

**arXiv:** [2611.16935v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16935v1)

**Submitted:** 2026-11-12

**Keyword hits:** foundation model

**Score:** 1

**Abstract:**

Public health agencies need to anticipate how outbreaks will unfold, when they will peak, and how long demand on health services will remain elevated. We previously developed a foundation model, Mantis, which was trained entirely on simulated outbreaks---enabling us to generate the diversity and scale of training data needed to support forecasting across diseases. However, our previous work left unresolved when simulation improves predictions and how it should complement observational data.
  Here, we varied the balance of simulated and observed training data while holding forecasting tasks fixed and varying the available surveillance history. We found that simulation-rich training was particularly valuable with limited local history, and its relative advantage diminished as more observations became available. We also found that simulation-based training reduced exceptionally large forecast errors, suggesting that mechanistic knowledge can help compensate for limited local evidence.
  Building on these findings, we developed Mantis 2, which combines simulated and observational training to jointly forecast up to six variables sixteen weeks ahead. Across 30 forecasting tasks, Mantis 2 achieved the best aggregate probabilistic accuracy over short, medium, and long horizons among the machine learning, statistical, and baseline models evaluated. It missed roughly 30\% fewer outbreak peaks than the strongest comparison model, with successfully anticipated peaks identified more than six weeks in advance on average. We publicly release Mantis 2, enabling public health agencies and researchers to forecast disease outcomes without developing a separate model for each disease or location.

## MutAtlas: A PDB-Wide Energy-Guided Atlas of Protein Mutation Effects

**Authors:** Ruihan Guo, Chaoran Cheng, Zhanghan Ni, Neil He, Bangji Yang, Ge Liu

**Primary category:** cs.LG

**arXiv:** [2611.16361v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16361v1)

**Submitted:** 2026-11-12

**Keyword hits:** variant interpretation

**Score:** 1

**Abstract:**

Protein mutation effect prediction is fundamental to protein engineering and disease variant interpretation, yet experimentally measured mutation data remain accurate but extremely sparse. To provide scalable supplementary mutation signals, we construct a PDB-wide mutation augmentation dataset that exhaustively enumerates single-site substitutions on experimentally resolved protein structures and aligns mutation signals from physics-based energy models, protein language models, and inverse folding models. Large-scale analysis under a unified mutation preference representation reveals substantial differences in the consistency, concentration, and substitution patterns of mutation distributions across models, indicating that disagreement is pervasive and reflects conflicting inductive biases rather than random noise. Motivated by these observations, we propose an unsupervised multi-source mutation preference distillation framework that learns from relative mutation preferences while explicitly modeling cross-source disagreement. Without using any experimental mutation labels during training, our approach achieves the best overall performance among the evaluated zero-shot baselines and naive multi-source fusion strategies on ProteinGym. We release the dataset and evaluation pipeline to support reproducible studies of protein mutation effects.

## From More to Better: Causal Inference and Digital Experimentation in the Age of AI

**Authors:** Winston Chou, Mårten Schultzberg, Lo-Hua Yuan, Wenjing Zheng, Avi Feller, David Holtz, Widad Machmouchi, Sven Schmit, Gagan Bhat, Simon Ejdemyr, Veronica Hannan, Amy Hartwig, Mahnaz Islam, Nathan Kallus, Jeremy Karp, Kyle Kretschman, Hannah Li, James McQueen, Evan Miller, Nicolas Stier, Sean Taylor

**Primary category:** cs.AI

**arXiv:** [2611.16335v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16335v1)

**Submitted:** 2026-11-12

**Keyword hits:** causal inference

**Score:** 1

**Abstract:**

Artificial intelligence (AI) is quickly expanding the capacity of organizations to produce and analyze data. We argue that this expansion shifts the leverage of data scientists from performing individual analyses to assessing the credibility and importance of findings and determining when evidence generalizes across contexts. Drawing on a roundtable of academic researchers and industry practitioners, we examine three ways in which data scientists who work on causal inference and experimentation can help organizations turn more analytical capacity into better decisions: preserving and accumulating the learning value of experimentation beyond individual tests; choosing and combining experimental, observational, and synthetic evidence; and ensuring evaluation keeps pace with AI as its capabilities and uses evolve. In each area, we distinguish established practice from open questions. We conclude with implications for the training and practice of data science and for organizations.

## Robot-Induced Bias in Movement Smoothness Metrics for Rehabilitation: Quantification and Correction in Simulation

**Authors:** David Regev, Alex Scherbakov, Eran Beeri Bamani

**Primary category:** cs.RO

**arXiv:** [2611.15743v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15743v1)

**Submitted:** 2026-11-11

**Keyword hits:** motor

**Score:** 1

**Abstract:**

Robot mechanics can bias kinematic measures of recovery and limit comparisons across rehabilitation devices. We developed a closed-loop human-robot simulation calibrated to published healthy and stroke group kinematics. Paired runs held motor-control parameters and movement plans fixed to quantify bias in seven metrics relative to a low-impedance reference, normalized using external repeatability benchmarks. Within a subset informed by published device specifications, median log dimensionless jerk (LDLJ) bias was 0.93 and 1.79 benchmark units for stroke and healthy profiles, respectively. Sobol analysis identified Coulomb friction and apparent mass as leading contributors; LDLJ bias persisted across 11 robustness variants. LDLJ bias increased as simulated movements became less fragmented. If recovery similarly reduces fragmentation, this effect could partly mask improvement in measured smoothness. Correction combining identified robot dynamics and observed metrics with gradient-boosted trees reduced median normalized absolute error from 0.77 to 0.10 for LDLJ and from 0.24 to 0.08 for spectral arc length (SPARC) in the device-informed subset of a random 20% hold-out. Median smoothness residuals remained at or below 0.15 benchmark units across four separately held-out parameter regions. Under an alternative controller, LDLJ error decreased from 1.60 to 0.17 without retraining, although transfer varied across metrics. These simulation results support mechanical characterization and movement-dependent correction toward more comparable robotic assessments, motivating validation in patients.
